Best high-yield savings accounts: rates, tiers and the 6-point checklist

Rates move. Promotions expire. Sponsors change. Start with the baseline: what the average saver earns

Before you look at any offer, know the floor. The FDIC publishes the national average savings rate every week, and it is the single best test of whether an offer is serious.

When we last checked, that national average sat around 0.41% APY, while leading online accounts advertised roughly 4.0% to 5.0% APY. That is not a small difference. On $10,000, one is about $41 a year. The other is several hundred.

Check the live number before you actThe FDIC figure moves weekly. Open FDIC national rates and rate caps in another tab and use today's number, not ours. Our figure is stamped October 10, 2026.

One clarification on terminology, because it trips people up: FDIC-insured savings accounts always quote APY, not APR. APY includes the effect of compounding, which is what you actually earn. If a provider advertises an APR, convert it before comparing. Comparing APR to APY is comparing two different things.

What the APY banner does not tell you

APY is the right number to compare. But it is also variable, and the headline version is often not what you will earn all year.

Promotional windows

A big rate that lasts 60 or 90 days, then drops to a lower standard rate. The drop lives in the footnotes.

Balance tiers and caps

The advertised rate may only apply up to a cap, sometimes as low as the first $1,000 or $10,000. Anything above earns less. A “3% rate” that only covers $1,000 is closer to a $30 bonus than a 3% account.

Behaviour-linked rates

Some accounts require direct deposit, a minimum number of debit purchases, or a linked checking account. Miss one month and the rate slips without an email.

Do not ask which bank pays the most today. Ask which bank pays a sensible standard rate on your balance, with your habits, six months from now.

The promo-rate trap, with real math

Imagine two banks.

Bank A advertises 5.00%. The footnote says that rate lasts three months, then drops to 3.50%.

Bank B pays a flat 4.30%. No promotion, no conditions.

Which one pays more over a year? Bank B. Comfortably.

Here is the calculation you can reuse for any offer you find:

blended APY = (promo rate x promo months + standard rate x remaining months) / 12 Bank A = (5.00 x 3 + 3.50 x 9) / 12 = 3.88% Bank B = 4.30%

If finding the standard rate takes more than two minutes of digging, treat that as information too.

Fees: the silent APY killer

Fees work like interest in reverse. On smaller balances, they win.

Take $2,000. Moving from near-zero to a 4.25% rate earns you roughly $85 extra a year. Add a $5 monthly maintenance fee and $60 of that disappears.

Fees to check before you open

  • Monthly maintenance, and the conditions to waive it
  • Inactivity or low-balance fees
  • Excess withdrawal or transfer fees
  • Outgoing wire and expedited transfer fees

Rule of thumb: for an emergency fund, the target fee is zero.

FDIC insurance, decoded

FDIC insurance protects deposits at insured banks up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. If the bank fails, insured money is protected.

Two quick checks confirm you are covered:

  1. The institution is an insured bank. Search it on FDIC BankFind.
  2. Your account is held at that bank. Not just marketed by an app that partners with it.

The fintech app question

Many money apps are not banks. They place your deposits at partner banks. That can be perfectly fine, as long as the app clearly names the bank so you can verify it.

Red flag“Funds held at our partner bank” with no name. If you cannot verify the bank, you cannot verify the insurance.

The 30-minute method

  1. Minutes 0 to 10. Get the live numbers. Open the FDIC rate page and one rankings site. Write down the national average and the top five offers.
  2. Minutes 10 to 20. Copy the facts from each bank. From their own site: current APY, standard APY after any promo, fee schedule, transfer rules, minimum balance.
  3. Minutes 20 to 28. Score them. Use the six checks. Double the weight on fees and insurance.
  4. Minutes 28 to 30. Act. Open the winner, move a small test amount first, then automate a monthly transfer.
Set a reminderReview once a year, or whenever rates move sharply. Banks count on inertia, and a yearly check is free money.

Five mistakes that cost real money

  • Chasing the top rate at an app that will not name its bank
  • Ignoring a fee that cancels out the interest
  • Treating a promotional rate as permanent
  • Moving everything before testing a small transfer
  • Never checking the rate again after opening the account

Frequently asked questions

What is a good high-yield savings rate right now?

Compare against the FDIC national average, which moves weekly. A rate well above the national average and available on your full balance, with no fee, is competitive. Use the FDIC link above for today's figure rather than trusting any article, including this one.

Why do you not list specific banks?

Because rates change weekly and a ranking published today is wrong in a month. We give you the live sources and the comparison method instead, which stay accurate.

Is a high APY guaranteed?

No. Savings rates are variable and can change at any time. Only a CD locks a rate, and only for its term.

Are online banks safe?

If they are FDIC-insured banks, deposits are protected up to the insurance limits. Verify the institution on FDIC BankFind.

How much should I keep in a high-yield savings account?

A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. Our emergency fund runway calculator gives you a personal number.

Is interest taxable?

Yes. Savings interest is generally taxable income in the US. Banks report it on a 1099-INT when it passes the reporting threshold.
