The Pattern Day Trader rule, before anything else

In the US, a margin account that makes four or more day trades within five business days is treated as a pattern day trader. It must then maintain at least $25,000 in equity.

Below that threshold, the broker restricts further day trades. This is not a policy you can negotiate. It is regulation.

Practically, a small account cannot legally day trade frequently. Any platform suggesting otherwise is selling you a limitation you will discover on your fourth trade of the week.

Zero commission is not free

This is the most expensive misunderstanding in retail trading.

A broker advertising zero commissions still earns from your activity elsewhere. And you still pay costs that never appear on a commission line.

What you actually pay

  • Regulatory fees on sales
  • Per-contract fees on options, even at “commission-free” brokers
  • Monthly data subscriptions for real-time quotes and advanced charting
  • The bid-ask spread, which widens in volatile markets when you most want to trade
all_in_cost = commissions + options fees + regulatory fees + (data subscription x months) + (average spread x shares traded) rules of thumb: count the data subscription as fixed, before you trade a single share spreads widen exactly when you are most tempted to trade

A trader making a hundred round trips a month on a modest account can pay more in spreads and data fees than a commission schedule would ever have cost.

Execution quality: the factor nobody compares

Two brokers can show the same price and give different fills.

Order routing, whether the broker sells your order flow, and how it handles fast markets all affect the price you actually get.

Read the broker's published order-routing and payment-for-order-flow disclosures. Look for independent execution-quality studies rather than marketing claims.

On a quiet market this hardly matters. At a volatile open, it is the difference between a strategy that works and one that bleeds.

Taxes: the part that surprises most people

Profits held less than a year are short-term capital gains. In the US they are generally taxed at ordinary income rates, not the lower long-term rate.

Frequent trading also creates paperwork. Wash-sale rules can disallow losses if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days. High trade counts make cost basis tedious to reconstruct.

Before you trade actively, understand how your platform reports gains and losses. Then decide whether your tax situation makes frequent trading even less attractive than the raw numbers suggest.

When costs, spreads and short-term taxes all work against you, the strategy must be exceptional just to break even.

A saner default for most readers

For money you want to grow over years rather than days, the boring approach wins for most households.

A diversified low-cost portfolio held long-term, plus an emergency fund in an insured savings account, covers most people well.

Frequently asked questions

Is day trading a way to save money?

No. Trading is risk-taking, not saving. Use money you can afford to lose entirely.

Do zero-commission brokers mean trading is free?

No. Regulatory fees, options fees, data subscriptions and spreads all still apply. Spreads often dwarf commissions.

What is the Pattern Day Trader rule?

A US rule: a margin account making four or more day trades in five business days must maintain at least $25,000 in equity.

How are profits taxed?

Gains held under a year are generally short-term capital gains, taxed at ordinary income rates. Wash-sale rules may disallow certain losses.

Should I paper trade first?

Yes, and for longer than feels comfortable. Paper trading reveals whether the strategy survives costs and your discipline.
