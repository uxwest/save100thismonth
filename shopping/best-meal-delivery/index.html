Best Meal Delivery: Kits vs Prepared vs Restaurant Apps

Food is the most elastic line in most household budgets, and delivery is where it stretches fastest. A restaurant delivery order is not just the menu price — it is a marked-up menu price, plus a service fee, plus a delivery fee, plus a tip on top of all three. A meal kit costs more per serving than cooking from scratch but less than delivery, while prepared meals cost more again and save all the prep. This guide does the arithmetic honestly, names the hidden costs, and helps you match a model to how your actual week works.

The pattern is consistent across markets: batch cooking from groceries is the cheapest per serving by a wide margin, meal kits sit in the middle, prepared meals cost more, and restaurant delivery is the most expensive once every fee and tip is counted. The question is never “which is cheapest” but “which do I actually follow through on,” because a cheap plan you abandon is the most expensive plan of all.

Working the numbers for a real week

Take a household of two eating four dinners a week that come from one of these sources. That is sixteen dinners a month. Even allowing for generous ranges, the direction is unmistakeable. Cutting restaurant delivery from four nights a week to once a month, and covering the rest with batch cooking and an occasional kit, is one of the largest single levers available in a normal household budget. It is not about never ordering in. It is about noticing that the default has quietly become the expensive option.

Fees and markups to look for specifically

  • Menu markup. Compare the app's price against the restaurant's own menu. A silent increase of twenty to thirty percent is common, and you are paying it before any fee appears.
  • Service fee. Often a percentage, sometimes described as going to the platform rather than the restaurant.
  • Delivery fee. Varies by distance and demand, and rises at exactly the times you want to order.
  • Small-order fee. Applied when your basket is below a threshold the app chooses.
  • Tip. Expected on top of everything above, and now frequently defaulted to a percentage of the inflated total.
  • Subscription “free delivery” programs, which only pay off above a specific order frequency — calculate your own break-even before enrolling.
The quiet trap: a delivery subscription can make ordering feel free, which increases order frequency. If you order twice as often because delivery is “already paid for,” the subscription has cost you money rather than saved it.

Meal kits: worth it, and for whom

Meal kits solve a specific problem: deciding what to cook and shopping for it. If those two tasks are what stop you cooking, the per-serving premium over groceries is buying something real. What it does not do is beat groceries on price, and the promotional pricing is temporary. Before committing, find the skip-week deadline in your account settings, read what happens if you miss it, and confirm the post-promotion price. The single most useful piece of information for a meal kit subscriber is the date by which they must act each week.

Prepared meals: paying for time, honestly

Prepared meals save the cooking as well as the planning, which is why they cost more per serving. They make sense when your time is genuinely scarce and expensive, or when specific dietary requirements would otherwise mean ordering out. Watch for portion sizes that leave you hungry, subscription minimums that lock you into more meals than you eat, and the difference between “fresh” and “frozen” ranges, which is often simply a price difference rather than a quality one.

A practical hybrid that most households can sustain

Rather than picking one model forever, set a default and carve out exceptions. Batch cook on a normal week. Keep one meal kit for the weeks you know will be chaotic. Allow restaurant delivery once a month, deliberately, as a treat rather than a fallback. This structure keeps the savings without demanding the discipline that makes people abandon strict plans entirely. Set it up once and the expensive option stops being automatic.

Frequently asked questions

Are meal kits cheaper than buying groceries?

Rarely on a pure per-serving basis. Their value is the time saved on planning and shopping and the food waste avoided. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how you actually behave, not on the sticker price alone.

How do I cancel a meal kit subscription cleanly?

Find the weekly cutoff date in your account settings and pause or cancel before it. That cutoff is the most important single detail for any kit subscriber, and it is usually buried.

Is delivery-app food more expensive than ordering directly?

Usually yes. Compare the app's menu price with the restaurant's own menu, then add service fee, delivery fee and tip. Ordering directly and collecting often costs considerably less for the same food.

Is a delivery subscription worth it?

Only if you order frequently enough that the waived fees exceed the subscription cost — and only if it does not cause you to order more often than you otherwise would.

What is the single biggest saving available here?

Reducing the frequency of restaurant delivery, because it is consistently the most expensive per meal once all fees and tips are counted. Cutting it from weekly to monthly is often the largest single change.
