Best Antivirus for Mac: Do You Actually Need One?

Ask a Mac owner whether they need antivirus and you will get two confident, opposite answers. Both can be right, because the honest answer depends entirely on how you use the machine. macOS ships with layered, genuinely effective protection, and a careful user who installs software from trusted sources may never need to pay a subscription. A household where several people install whatever they find, or where someone handles sensitive accounts on a shared machine, is a different case. This guide explains what Apple already gives you, when third-party software earns its price, and how to compare products without falling for fear-based pricing.

What macOS already protects you from

Gatekeeper verifies apps before their first launch and blocks unsigned or tampered software by default, which is why the “cannot be opened because the developer cannot be verified” message appears. XProtect is Apple's built-in malware signature system, updated independently of full system updates. Sandboxing limits what a compromised app can reach, and system integrity protection prevents modification of critical system files even by software running with high privileges.

This combination is not a token gesture. It is why the classic “Macs get no viruses” belief persists, and why many careful users genuinely do not need additional software. The important caveat is that these protections are configurable and can be bypassed by a user who clicks through warnings, and they are not primarily designed to stop phishing, which is the attack most likely to cost you money.

Verify on Apple's own documentation. Apple publishes current descriptions of Gatekeeper, XProtect and sandboxing, so you can confirm what is active on your version of macOS rather than relying on a list like this one.

When third-party antivirus earns its price on a Mac

  • You install software from outside the App Store and from sources you do not control or verify.
  • The Mac is shared with family members whose clicking habits you cannot supervise.
  • You want browser-level phishing protection and web filtering on top of malware scanning, which is where paid suites differentiate most.
  • You run Windows or other operating systems in a virtual machine on the same computer, where the threat profile differs.
  • You handle high-value accounts — business banking, client data — where a small extra layer is proportionate to the loss it might prevent.
“Buy extra protection when it addresses your actual behaviour, not when an advertisement says your Mac is unprotected.”

The free habits that beat most paid tools

For the money you would spend on a subscription, these actions prevent more real-world harm: keep macOS fully updated so XProtect and system patches are current; install applications only from the App Store or a developer's official download page; use a password manager so you never reuse credentials; turn on two-factor authentication on email, banking and any account that can reset the others; and treat unexpected urgency — a threatening email, a pop-up warning, a call claiming to be support — as a phishing attempt by default. An email account is the master key to everything else, so protecting it is worth more than any scan.

A specific, common trap: fake “your Mac is infected, call this number” pop-ups and browser notifications. Genuine security software does not ask you to phone a support line from a web page. Force-quit the browser rather than clicking anything in the message.

A decision rule you can apply in two minutes

If you install software only from trusted sources, keep the system updated, and nobody else uses the machine, you are likely fine with what macOS provides and the habits above. If any of those is false, or you specifically want phishing and web filtering as a paid feature, choose the product with strong independent Mac-specific results, tolerable performance impact and a renewal price you are willing to pay for three years — then verify that price before you subscribe. And if you already pay for a bundle that includes security, check what it covers before buying more.

Frequently asked questions

Can Macs get viruses?

Yes. Malware and unwanted software exist for macOS, though the profile differs from Windows and the built-in protections stop much of it. The most common real-world risk for most users is phishing, not a classic virus.

Is the built-in protection enough?

For careful users who install software only from trusted sources, usually yes. For riskier usage or shared machines, third-party software adds a defensive layer, particularly for web and phishing protection.

Why is antivirus suddenly warning me over the phone or in a pop-up?

That is almost certainly a scam. Reputable security software does not ask you to call a support number from a browser warning. Close the browser without clicking anything inside the message.

Do I need a VPN too?

Not automatically. A VPN is useful on untrusted networks and for privacy from your network operator, but many people already pay for one through their employer or another bundle. Check before adding it to a security subscription.

How often should I review my security subscriptions?

Annually, before renewal. Ask whether the threat you bought protection for is still your situation, and whether you are paying twice for overlapping features.

Sources and how to verify

Confirm current built-in protections in Apple's own documentation for your version of macOS, and rely on independent, Mac-specific lab testing when comparing paid products.
